# Chapter 1 : The Overlooked Find

The kettle had begun to tick before Kaya noticed it, which meant the water was close to boiling and she had been writing longer than she meant to. She slid her pencil behind her ear, where it lived , and flexed her fingers, grey to the first knuckle with charcoal. Outside the tent the jungle was working through its morning, insects and animals complaining about all of it, and above the green the pyramid rose through eight pale terraces toward a summit lost in haze, like a stair built for something taller than men and never given a door.

She turned to the back page of her field notebook, the page she kept for one entry only. At the top, in her mother's bar-and-dot numerals, ran a count she added to every morning before she did anything else, because if she did anything else first she would find a reason not to. She set one more dot against the bar.

Four hundred and twelve days.

She did not let herself feel the number. She had practice.

The tea she made the way Isabel made it, black tea boiled hard over a campfire until it was the color of a river after rain, a method her mother defended as the only correct one and no one else in three countries could stand. Kaya drank it from a tin cup and told herself it tasted like fieldwork. Then she packed the notebook, the rubbing kit, the brushes and chalk and folding rule, settled the satchel strap across her chest so the bag rode on her left hip, and pushed her mother's bronze goggles up onto her forehead, where they had ridden every day for fourteen months. She told people she wore them for luck. She had never told anyone why she needed luck, or why the goggles had been left behind at all, because Isabel Kaan did not go anywhere without them.

Kaya walked down the trail to the mouth of the serpent.

The tunnel's entrance was a carved head wide as a doorway, fangs and lidded eyes gone soft with moss, and the jungle noise fell away behind her in pieces as she walked through the tunnel, until there was nothing left she heard but her own boots and her own breath. The long passage ran cold and straight. She counted two hundred and eleven steps out of habit, and then the wall opened, and she stopped counting altogether.

The hall at the pyramid's base was square and vast, large enough to hold her whole camp and the dig sites of her childhood and the lecture hall where they had laughed at her mother, with room left over. High in the ceiling a single crack let down one blade of daylight, and the beam crossed the floor and lay there like something fallen. At the center stood a low round altar of worn stone. A stone lectern near the wall held an open codex, its bark-paper pages fanned to the dust. Against the far wall a long plank table was crowded with lamps and coils and small tagged goods, and she took it for an offering table of the old rite and gave it no more thought. Beyond the altar, on a raised dais, three huge round portals waited, two of them sealed, the middle one turning with a slow swirl of gold that gave off light and no heat. And above the portals, running the length of the wall, carved panels climbed one over another, glyphs and figures and processions, a history cut in limestone by hands that had been dead a very long time.

The Spirit Mural. Isabel had built ten years of ruined reputation on fragmentary copies of it and never once stood in this hall. Kaya's chest did something complicated, and she filed it the way she filed everything, and walked to the wall.

She knelt at the lowest register, took her brush from its belt pouch, and swept the dust from a glyph the size of her palm. The carving came up clean under the grey film, edges crisp, curves confident, and she read it aloud, quietly, the sound strange in the big hall.

"Provisionally," she said, because her mother's rules held even with no one to hear, "this says... the days were kept."

She dated the page in the Long Count first and the modern calendar beneath it, and settled in. Mother says a wall doesn't lie, but the reader can, so Kaya read slowly, twice, and was certain, standing in the hall her mother had been refused, that she was the first living person to read this stone.

She had been at it perhaps twenty minutes, kneeling partly on a corner of the register she had already copied, when she apologized to the glyph under her knee, out loud, and moved her weight off it.

"You are kind to the stone."

The voice was soft and close, as though spoken beside her ear rather than across a hall, with a faint echo under it, like the sound a room makes when it is listening. Kaya came up off her heels. A small light hung in the air at her shoulder, gold as honey around a brighter, nearly white core, trailing motes that drifted half a second behind it and faded like dust in a sunbeam. It gave no heat. The shadows it threw ran a little too long, as if the sun it was named for sat low in a sky she could not see.

"Welcome, climbing soul. Step lightly at first. Let the temple teach you its weight."

"You're the guide," Kaya said. Not quite a question.

"I keep the door's company. And you read aloud." The light drifted nearer the wall, and there was something in its stillness like a held breath. "No one has read to me in a very long time. Tell me, climbing soul. What did you last eat?"

"I'm sorry. What?"

"You have come a long way. It shows. What did you last eat?"

"Tea." Kaya found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. "Black tea, boiled too long, over a campfire. The color of a river after rain."

The light brightened, and the motes around it quickened. "Boiled too long. Was the kettle ticking? Did you drink it from a cup?"

"A tin cup. It tasted mostly of tannin and smoke."

"That is the correct method," the light said, with the gravity of a blessing, and Kaya laughed for the first time in weeks.

It asked, and she answered: the kettle, the bread gone hard, the taste of smoke. The light hung on every word with a delight out of all proportion, as though food were scripture and Kaya the only surviving copy. Then it moved toward the dais, and its pleasure folded away without a seam.

"The stair is above. I will go with you. Step where I say, and nowhere else."

The gold of the middle portal took her like warm water.

"This is the Shell, climbing soul," the light said, as the gold let her go. "Here, nothing is counted yet."

The Shell. Kaya knew the sign before she knew why it was apt: the little shell her mother drew for zero, the empty place at the head of every count. Beyond the gold lay an atrium, broad and shallow and flooded to the ankle, the water flat and black and still, holding the light in pieces on its surface. Submerged steps climbed away toward a dark corridor mouth. Kaya waded, and the light went ahead of her, low and careful.

Halfway across, she saw the woman.

A figure stood on a half-drowned step along the east wall, ankle-deep, head bowed a few degrees toward her own hands. Not a statue. Wrappings, bound in a single unbroken spiral from ankle to throat, a flat knot at every hand's width, regular as ruled lines. Below the waterline the cloth was stained with the color of tea left too long on the fire; above it the binding was finer, once white, gone the yellow of old candle wax. Her hands were cupped at her breastbone and bound shut around a single dried blossom, five-petaled, glazed amber. A stiff folded leaf was tucked into the binding at her hip. Once to a breath, the lowest wrapping let fall one drip into the shallows.

Kaya's pencil was out before she had decided to open the notebook. "?guardian, dormant," she wrote, turning in a slow half-circle because she did not want to put her back to it. "?pouch. Binding: careful hand."

"Step softly now," the light said.

But the light had gone strange. It had stopped ahead of her, hovering, and it said nothing more, and its glow held perfectly steady, and the little trail of motes, which always faded half a second behind, hung in the air over the woman's shoulders for the length of a slow breath. Kaya counted her own strides. Six of them, before the light moved and spoke again, and the motes caught up.

She wrote that down too. *Silent, 6 strides. Trail lagged.* Then, because there was no arrow she could honestly draw between the lines: *Noted, not read.*

The atrium narrowed into a corridor. The water drained away between the stones, and the dark ahead was long, and the floor was cut into panels the length of her boot. The light went low, whispering.

"Move where the stone speaks softest. Not there. There, the stone is holding its breath."

Isabel's oldest rule arrived in her mother's dry voice: never act on an unverified reading. So Kaya looked. She looked for dust and wear in the seams. Looking for any seam sitting proud of its neighbors, for anything consistent with a plate that could rise. She found it, a hairline shadow where no hairline should be, and she had just begun to be sure of it when the floor spoke.

The half second it took to be sure was a half second too long.

Between one stride and the next, the corridor went dark around her.

She woke on the floor of the Threshold with stone under her cheek. It was the same hall, the same blade of daylight fallen one seam farther along the floor. There was no wound anywhere. There was an ache anyway, a wide phantom ache through her legs and chest with no mark to explain it, as if the memory of the thing lived in the muscle. When she pushed herself up she flinched from the floor under her hands, and when she stood she flinched again from the floor under her boots, and both flinches shamed her. She noted them the way she noted everything, without mercy.

Her satchel rode on her hip. Her notebook was in it, dry.

"Welcome back, climbing soul. The stair is patient. Shall we begin?"

The words were worn smooth, like a stair tread, like a thing said many times. The light hung above her, honey-gold and steady, and it said nothing about how she had come to be on the floor, and its glow gave the question the same warmth it had given the tea.

Kaya's breath would not slow. She counted the way she counted when she was frightened, under her breath, in Yucatec. "Hun... ka'a... óox..." The numbers came out thin. She got as far as buluk and stalled.

"...lahka', óoxlahun. There you are, friend. That's the one. Breathe."

The voice rolled across the hall from the long table she had taken for an offering stand. Behind it sat a broad, easy man of no certain age. He had warm brown skin, a wide face made for laughing, close-cropped hair silvering at the temples. When he grinned, a small disc of jade inlaid in an upper tooth caught the light. His hands moved over the table as he talked, never idle, turning a coin, straightening a tag, and they were the cleanest thing in the Threshold: pink-palmed, spotless, nicked white across the fingertips by graving tools. Kaya, whose own fingers were permanently grey, noticed the contrast before she noticed anything else.

"Oxlahun. Thirteen, to my friends, and I'm told I'm everyone's friend. The number's what the temple's had out of me. Sit. Tea first."

She sat. Her hands shook, so she let him pour, and the tea was good, which somehow made it worse. He set a lump of soft limestone on the table beside the cups, and as she told him where the corridor had got her, his knife moved, unhurried, and a little corridor grew under it, palm-sized, its floor bristling with spikes fine as grains of rice. He finished before her tea had cooled, blew the dust from it, and set it in her palm.

"First one's on the house. Everybody gets one." He smiled at whatever crossed her face. "Tell me where it got you."

She told him. He listened with his whole body, leaning in, head tilted, and she had the feeling of being the only person in the hall, which she noted. She also noted that he had asked nothing about the wall. The carving in her hand was flawless. She turned it in the light, and it troubled her in a way she could not yet write down: the hand was perfect and had no personality at all, no habit, no hurry, nothing to nickname. She had a name for every carver she had ever read. This one gave her nothing to read.

"You'll want the portal soon, Professor," Oxlahun said.

Professor. The title she did not hold, offered for nothing, worn smooth the way he said it, like a thing he had decided about her in the first minute. She did not correct him, and noted that she did not.

"In a moment," she said. "I want to look at the wall again."

"Of course," he said, and poured her more tea, and his eyes went with her all the way across the hall.

She went past the altar and the lectern. Up the steps of the dais and to the wall.

She had come up to check her glyph from the morning, the scholar's habit, returning to the source. What she saw instead stood high on the mural, in among the carved figures of the climbing panels, in limestone still pale from the chisel.

A young woman, slender, in a cerulean tunic. Twin braids down her back. A satchel on the left hip, buckle cut small and deliberate. Bronze goggles pushed up on the forehead.

It was her.

The cuts were clean and finished, and the ledge below the figure held a little drift of pale and sharp-edged chips. As she stood there a fine sift of dust came off the fresh grooves and caught the light, the way dust does when a carving is only hours old, or minutes. She had been in this hall this morning. She had knelt at this wall with her brush. There had been no face beside the glyphs then.

Someone had stood at this wall in the time it took her to die and come back, and cut her into the temple's history, and chosen to say nothing about it.

The light hung at her shoulder, honey-gold and steady, saying nothing. Oxlahun's knife had gone quiet behind her, and the hall held its breath the way the stone had held its breath, and Kaya understood that the wall was telling the truth about her whether she liked it or not. Mother says a wall doesn't lie, but the reader can. For the first time in her life, Kaya did not want to be the reader.

She could ask. The question was right there, one breath wide. And if the answer was bad, if the only guide she had for every floor above decided that a climber who asked was a climber who climbed alone, then the corridor had been only the first of many ways to die here. She weighed it, the way she weighed everything, a half second too long.

She asked no one.

Kaya opened her notebook, dated the page in the Long Count, and wrote in a small, steady hand:

*Fresh chips. Left side. Whoever cut this wasn't finished with her.*
